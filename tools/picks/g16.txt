# Occurrence
205+206 Oxygen, sulphur, selenium, tellurium, polonium and livermorium constitute Group 16 of the periodic table. This is sometimes known as group of chalcogens.
207+208 The name is derived from the Greek word for brass and points to the association of sulphur and its congeners with copper. Most copper minerals contain either oxygen or sulphur and frequently the other members of the group.
211 Oxygen is the most abundant of all the elements on earth.
212 Oxygen forms about 46.6% by mass of earth's crust. Dry air contains 20.946% oxygen by volume.
213 However, the abundance of sulphur in the earth's crust is only 0.03-0.1%.
214 Combined sulphur exists primarily as sulphates such as gypsum CaSO4.2H2O, epsom salt MgSO4.7H2O, baryte BaSO4 and sulphides such as galena PbS, zinc blende ZnS, copper pyrites CuFeS2.
215 Traces of sulphur occur as hydrogen sulphide in volcanoes.
216 Organic materials such as eggs, proteins, garlic, onion, mustard, hair and wool contain sulphur.
217 Selenium and tellurium are also found as metal selenides and tellurides in sulphide ores.
218 Polonium occurs in nature as a decay product of thorium and uranium minerals.
219 Livermorium is a synthetic radioactive element.
# Configuration, radii, ionisation and electron gain enthalpy
223 The elements of Group16 have six electrons in the outermost shell and have ns^2 np^4 general electronic configuration.
224 Due to increase in the number of shells, atomic and ionic radii increase from top to bottom in the group.
225! The size of oxygen atom is, however, exceptionally small.
227+228 Ionisation enthalpy decreases down the group. It is due to increase in size.
229+230! However, the elements of this group have lower ionisation enthalpy values compared to those of Group15 in the corresponding periods. This is due to the fact that Group 15 elements have extra stable halffilled p orbitals electronic configurations.
231+232! Because of the compact nature of oxygen atom, it has less negative electron gain enthalpy than sulphur. However, from sulphur onwards the value again becomes less negative upto polonium.
226T Table 7.6: Electron gain enthalpy ∆egH/kJ mol^–1: O –141, S –200, Se –195, Te –190, Po –174. Electronegativity: O 3.50, S 2.58, Se 2.55, Te 2.01, Po 1.76.
233 Next to fluorine, oxygen has the highest electronegativity value amongst the elements.
234+235 Within the group, electronegativity decreases with an increase in atomic number. This implies that the metallic character increases from oxygen to polonium.
# Physical properties
237 Oxygen and sulphur are non-metals, selenium and tellurium metalloids, whereas polonium is a metal. Polonium is radioactive and is short lived (Half-life 13.8 days).
238 All these elements exhibit allotropy.
239 The melting and boiling points increase with an increase in atomic number down the group.
240 The large difference between the melting and boiling points of oxygen and sulphur may be explained on the basis of their atomicity; oxygen exists as diatomic molecule (O2) whereas sulphur exists as polyatomic molecule (S8).
# Oxidation states
242+243! The stability of -2 oxidation state decreases down the group. Polonium hardly shows –2 oxidation state.
244! Since electronegativity of oxygen is very high, it shows only negative oxidation state as –2 except in the case of OF2 where its oxidation state is + 2.
225! Oxygen shows oxidation states of +2 and +1 in oxygen fluorides OF2 and O2F2 respectively.
245 Other elements of the group exhibit + 2, + 4, + 6 oxidation states but + 4 and + 6 are more common.
246 Sulphur, selenium and tellurium usually show + 4 oxidation state in their compounds with oxygen and + 6 with fluorine.
247 The stability of + 6 oxidation state decreases down the group and stability of + 4 oxidation state increases (inert pair effect).
248 Bonding in +4 and +6 oxidation states is primarily covalent.
# Anomalous behaviour of oxygen
249! The anomalous behaviour of oxygen, like other members of p-block present in second period is due to its small size and high electronegativity.
250! One typical example of effects of small size and high electronegativity is the presence of strong hydrogen bonding in H2O which is not found in H2S.
269! The absence of d orbitals in oxygen limits its covalency to four and in practice, rarely exceeds two.
270 On the other hand, in case of other elements of the group, the valence shells can be expanded and covalence exceeds four.
# Hydrides H2E
271 All the elements of Group 16 form hydrides of the type H2E (E = O, S, Se, Te, Po).
273 Their acidic character increases from H2O to H2Te. The increase in acidic character can be explained in terms of decrease in bond enthalpy for the dissociation of H–E bond down the group.
274 Owing to the decrease in enthalpy for the dissociation of H–E bond down the group, the thermal stability of hydrides also decreases from H2O to H2Po.
274! All the hydrides except water possess reducing property and this character increases from H2S to H2Te.

# Oxides EO2, EO3
253 All these elements form oxides of the EO2 and EO3 types where E = S, Se, Te or Po. Ozone (O3) and sulphur dioxide (SO2) are gases while selenium dioxide (SeO2) is solid.
254! Reducing property of dioxide decreases from SO2 to TeO2; SO2 is reducing while TeO2 is an oxidising agent.
255+256 Besides EO2 type, sulphur, selenium and tellurium also form EO3 type oxides (SO3, SeO3, TeO3). Both types of oxides are acidic in nature.
# Halides
257 Elements of Group 16 form a large number of halides of the type, EX6, EX4 and EX2 where E is an element of the group and X is a halogen.
258 The stability of the halides decreases in the order F^– > Cl^– > Br^– > I^–.
259+260+261 Amongst hexahalides, hexafluorides are the only stable halides. All hexafluorides are gaseous in nature. They have octahedral structure.
262! Sulphur hexafluoride, SF6 is exceptionally stable for steric reasons.
263 Amongst tetrafluorides, SF4 is a gas, SeF4 a liquid and TeF4 a solid.
264+265 These fluorides have sp^3d hybridisation and thus, have trigonal bipyramidal structures in which one of the equatorial positions is occupied by a lone pair of electrons. This geometry is also regarded as see-saw geometry.
266! All elements except oxygen form dichlorides and dibromides.
267 These dihalides are formed by sp^3 hybridisation and thus, have tetrahedral structure.
268+275 The well known monohalides are dimeric in nature. Examples are S2F2, S2Cl2, S2Br2, Se2Cl2 and Se2Br2.
276 These dimeric halides undergo disproportionation as given below: 2Se2Cl2 → SeCl4 + 3Se
# Dioxygen
276 Dioxygen can be obtained in the laboratory by the following ways: (i) By heating oxygen containing salts such as chlorates, nitrates and permanganates.
277T 2KClO3 → 2KCl + 3O2 (heat, MnO2)
277 (ii) By the thermal decomposition of the oxides of metals low in the electrochemical series and higher oxides of some metals. 2Ag2O(s) → 4Ag(s) + O2(g); 2Pb3O4(s) → 6PbO(s) + O2(g); 2HgO(s) → 2Hg(l) + O2(g); 2PbO2(s) → 2PbO(s) + O2(g)
277 (iii) Hydrogen peroxide is readily decomposed into water and dioxygen by catalysts such as finely divided metals and manganese dioxide. 2H2O2(aq) → 2H2O(l) + O2(g)
278 Electrolysis of water leads to the release of hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode.
279 Industrially, dioxygen is obtained from air by first removing carbon dioxide and water vapour and then, the remaining gases are liquefied and fractionally distilled to give dinitrogen and dioxygen.
280 Dioxygen is a colourless and odourless gas.
281 Its solubility in water is to the extent of 3.08 cm^3 in 100 cm^3 water at 293 K which is just sufficient for the vital support of marine and aquatic life.
282 It liquefies at 90 K and freezes at 55 K.
282! Molecular oxygen, O2 is unique in being paramagnetic inspite of having even number of electrons.
283 Dioxygen directly reacts with nearly all metals and non-metals except some metals (e.g., Au, Pt) and some noble gases.
284 Its combination with other elements is often strongly exothermic which helps in sustaining the reaction.
285+298 However, to initiate the reaction, some external heating is required as bond dissociation enthalpy of oxgyen-oxygen double bond is high (493.4 kJ mol^–1).
299 Some of the reactions of dioxygen with metals, non-metals and other compounds are given below: 2Ca + O2 → 2CaO; 4Al + 3O2 → 2Al2O3; P4 + 5O2 → P4O10; C + O2 → CO2; 2ZnS + 3O2 → 2ZnO + 2SO2; CH4 + 2O2 → CO2 + 2H2O
299+300 Some compounds are catalytically oxidised. For example, 2SO2 + O2 → 2SO3 (V2O5); 4HCl + O2 → 2Cl2 + 2H2O (CuCl2)
300 In addition to its importance in normal respiration and combustion processes, oxygen is used in oxyacetylene welding, in the manufacture of many metals, particularly steel.
301 Oxygen cylinders are widely used in hospitals, high altitude flying and in mountaineering.
302 The combustion of fuels, e.g., hydrazines in liquid oxygen, provides tremendous thrust in rockets.
# Simple oxides
287 A binary compound of oxygen with another element is called oxide.
291 Oxides can be simple (e.g., MgO, Al2O3) or mixed (Pb3O4, Fe3O4).
292 Simple oxides can be classified on the basis of their acidic, basic or amphoteric character.
293 An oxide that combines with water to give an acid is termed acidic oxide (e.g., SO2, Cl2O7, CO2, N2O5).
294 For example, SO2 combines with water to give H2SO3, an acid. SO2 + H2O → H2SO3
294! As a general rule, only non-metal oxides are acidic but oxides of some metals in high oxidation state also have acidic character (e.g., Mn2O7, CrO3, V2O5).
295 The oxides which give a base with water are known as basic oxides (e.g., Na2O, CaO, BaO).
296+297 For example, CaO combines with water to give Ca(OH)2, a base. CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2
303 In general, metallic oxides are basic.
304+305+306 Some metallic oxides exhibit a dual behaviour. They show characteristics of both acidic as well as basic oxides. Such oxides are known as amphoteric oxides.
308 For example, Al2O3 reacts with acids as well as alkalies.
309T Al2O3(s) + 6HCl(aq) + 9H2O(l) → 2[Al(H2O)6]^3+(aq) + 6Cl^–(aq); Al2O3(s) + 6NaOH(aq) + 3H2O(l) → 2Na3[Al(OH)6](aq)
309+310+311 There are some oxides which are neither acidic nor basic. Such oxides are known as neutral oxides. Examples of neutral oxides are CO, NO and N2O.
# Ozone
311+312 Ozone is an allotropic form of oxygen. It is too reactive to remain for long in the atmosphere at sea level.
313+314 At a height of about 20 kilometres, it is formed from atmospheric oxygen in the presence of sunlight. This ozone layer protects the earth's surface from an excessive concentration of ultraviolet (UV) radiations.
315 When a slow dry stream of oxygen is passed through a silent electrical discharge, conversion of oxygen to ozone (10%) occurs.
316 The product is known as ozonised oxygen. 3O2 → 2O3 ∆H(298 K) = +142 kJ mol^–1
316 Since the formation of ozone from oxygen is an endothermic process, it is necessary to use a silent electrical discharge in its preparation to prevent its decomposition.
317 If concentrations of ozone greater than 10 per cent are required, a battery of ozonisers can be used, and pure ozone (b.p. 101.1K) can be condensed in a vessel surrounded by liquid oxygen.
318 Pure ozone is a pale blue gas, dark blue liquid and violet-black solid.
319+320 Ozone has a characteristic smell and in small concentrations it is harmless. However, if the concentration rises above about 100 parts per million, breathing becomes uncomfortable resulting in headache and nausea.
321+322 Ozone is thermodynamically unstable with respect to oxygen since its decomposition into oxygen results in the liberation of heat (∆H is negative) and an increase in entropy (∆S is positive). These two effects reinforce each other, resulting in large negative Gibbs energy change (∆G) for its conversion into oxygen.
323 It is not really surprising, therefore, high concentrations of ozone can be dangerously explosive.
324 Due to the ease with which it liberates atoms of nascent oxygen (O3 → O2 + O), it acts as a powerful oxidising agent.
325+326 For example, it oxidises lead sulphide to lead sulphate and iodide ions to iodine. PbS(s) + 4O3(g) → PbSO4(s) + 4O2(g); 2I^–(aq) + H2O(l) + O3(g) → 2OH^–(aq) + I2(s) + O2(g)
326+327 When ozone reacts with an excess of potassium iodide solution buffered with a borate buffer (pH 9.2), iodine is liberated which can be titrated against a standard solution of sodium thiosulphate. This is a quantitative method for estimating O3 gas.
342 Experiments have shown that nitrogen oxides (particularly nitrogen monoxide) combine very rapidly with ozone and there is, thus, the possibility that nitrogen oxides emitted from the exhaust systems of supersonic jet aeroplanes might be slowly depleting the concentration of the ozone layer in the upper atmosphere.
343 NO(g) + O3(g) → NO2(g) + O2(g)
343 Another threat to this ozone layer is probably posed by the use of freons which are used in aerosol sprays and as refrigerants.
344 The two oxygen-oxygen bond lengths in the ozone molecule are identical (128 pm) and the molecule is angular as expected with a bond angle of about 117°.
345 It is a resonance hybrid of two main forms.
346 It is used as a germicide, disinfectant and for sterilising water.
347 It is also used for bleaching oils, ivory, flour, starch, etc. It acts as an oxidising agent in the manufacture of potassium permanganate.
# Sulphur: allotropic forms
328 Sulphur forms numerous allotropes of which the yellow rhombic (α-sulphur) and monoclinic (β-sulphur) forms are the most important.
329 The stable form at room temperature is rhombic sulphur, which transforms to monoclinic sulphur when heated above 369 K.
329 Rhombic sulphur (α-sulphur): This allotrope is yellow in colour, m.p. 385.8 K and specific gravity 2.06.
330 Rhombic sulphur crystals are formed on evaporating the solution of roll sulphur in CS2. It is insoluble in water but dissolves to some extent in benzene, alcohol and ether.
331 It is readily soluble in CS2.
331+332 Monoclinic sulphur (β-sulphur): Its m.p. is 393 K and specific gravity 1.98. It is soluble in CS2.
332+333+334 This form of sulphur is prepared by melting rhombic sulphur in a dish and cooling, till crust is formed. Two holes are made in the crust and the remaining liquid poured out. On removing the crust, colourless needle shaped crystals of β-sulphur are formed.
335+336 It is stable above 369 K and transforms into α-sulphur below it. Conversely, α-sulphur is stable below 369 K and transforms into β-sulphur above this.
337+338 At 369 K both the forms are stable. This temperature is called transition temperature.
339+340 Both rhombic and monoclinic sulphur have S8 molecules. These S8 molecules are packed to give different crystal structures.
341 The S8 ring in both the forms is puckered and has a crown shape.
350 Several other modifications of sulphur containing 6-20 sulphur atoms per ring have been synthesised in the last two decades.
351 In cyclo-S6, the ring adopts the chair form.
352! At elevated temperatures (~1000 K), S2 is the dominant species and is paramagnetic like O2.
349! In vapour state sulphur partly exists as S2 molecule which has two unpaired electrons in the antibonding π* orbitals like O2 and, hence, exhibits paramagnetism.
# Sulphur dioxide
352 Sulphur dioxide is formed together with a little (6-8%) sulphur trioxide when sulphur is burnt in air or oxygen: S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g)
352+353 In the laboratory it is readily generated by treating a sulphite with dilute sulphuric acid. SO3^2-(aq) + 2H^+(aq) → H2O(l) + SO2(g)
353 Industrially, it is produced as a by-product of the roasting of sulphide ores.
354T 4FeS2(s) + 11O2(g) → 2Fe2O3(s) + 8SO2(g)
354 The gas after drying is liquefied under pressure and stored in steel cylinders.
355 Sulphur dioxide is a colourless gas with pungent smell and is highly soluble in water.
356 It liquefies at room temperature under a pressure of two atmospheres and boils at 263 K.
356+357 Sulphur dioxide, when passed through water, forms a solution of sulphurous acid. SO2(g) + H2O(l) ⇌ H2SO3(aq)
357 It reacts readily with sodium hydroxide solution, forming sodium sulphite, which then reacts with more sulphur dioxide to form sodium hydrogen sulphite. 2NaOH + SO2 → Na2SO3 + H2O; Na2SO3 + H2O + SO2 → 2NaHSO3
357 In its reaction with water and alkalies, the behaviour of sulphur dioxide is very similar to that of carbon dioxide.
361+362 Sulphur dioxide reacts with chlorine in the presence of charcoal (which acts as a catalyst) to give sulphuryl chloride, SO2Cl2. SO2(g) + Cl2(g) → SO2Cl2(l)
362 It is oxidised to sulphur trioxide by oxygen in the presence of vanadium(V) oxide catalyst. 2SO2(g) + O2(g) → 2SO3(g) (V2O5)
362+363 When moist, sulphur dioxide behaves as a reducing agent. For example, it converts iron(III) ions to iron(II) ions and decolourises acidified potassium permanganate(VII) solution; the latter reaction is a convenient test for the gas.
363T 2Fe^3+ + SO2 + 2H2O → 2Fe^2+ + SO4^2– + 4H^+; 5SO2 + 2MnO4^– + 2H2O → 5SO4^2– + 4H^+ + 2Mn^2+
363+364 The molecule of SO2 is angular. It is a resonance hybrid of the two canonical forms.
364 Sulphur dioxide is used (i) in refining petroleum and sugar (ii) in bleaching wool and silk and (iii) as an anti-chlor, disinfectant and preservative.
365 Sulphuric acid, sodium hydrogen sulphite and calcium hydrogen sulphite (industrial chemicals) are manufactured from sulphur dioxide.
366 Liquid SO2 is used as a solvent to dissolve a number of organic and inorganic chemicals.
# Oxoacids of sulphur
358 Sulphur forms a number of oxoacids such as H2SO3, H2S2O3, H2S2O4, H2S2O5, H2SxO6 (x = 2 to 5), H2SO4, H2S2O7, H2SO5, H2S2O8.
359+360 Some of these acids are unstable and cannot be isolated. They are known in aqueous solution or in the form of their salts.
# Sulphuric acid
368 Sulphuric acid is one of the most important industrial chemicals worldwide.
369 Sulphuric acid is manufactured by the Contact Process which involves three steps: (i) burning of sulphur or sulphide ores in air to generate SO2. (ii) conversion of SO2 to SO3 by the reaction with oxygen in the presence of a catalyst (V2O5), and (iii) absorption of SO3 in H2SO4 to give Oleum (H2S2O7).
371 The SO2 produced is purified by removing dust and other impurities such as arsenic compounds.
372 The key step in the manufacture of H2SO4 is the catalytic oxidation of SO2 with O2 to give SO3 in the presence of V2O5 (catalyst).
373T 2SO2(g) + O2(g) → 2SO3(g) (V2O5); ∆rH = –196.6 kJ mol^–1
373+374+375 The reaction is exothermic, reversible and the forward reaction leads to a decrease in volume. Therefore, low temperature and high pressure are the favourable conditions for maximum yield. But the temperature should not be very low otherwise rate of reaction will become slow.
376 In practice, the plant is operated at a pressure of 2 bar and a temperature of 720 K.
376+377 The SO3 gas from the catalytic converter is absorbed in concentrated H2SO4 to produce oleum. Dilution of oleum with water gives H2SO4 of the desired concentration.
378 In the industry two steps are carried out simultaneously to make the process a continuous one and also to reduce the cost. SO3 + H2SO4 → H2S2O7 (Oleum)
378 The sulphuric acid obtained by Contact process is 96-98% pure.
378T Fig. 7.7: Arsenic purifier containing gelatinous hydrated ferric oxide.
381 Sulphuric acid is a colourless, dense, oily liquid with a specific gravity of 1.84 at 298 K. The acid freezes at 283 K and boils at 611 K. It dissolves in water with the evolution of a large quantity of heat.
382+383 Hence, care must be taken while preparing sulphuric acid solution from concentrated sulphuric acid. The concentrated acid must be added slowly into water with constant stirring.
384 The chemical reactions of sulphuric acid are as a result of the following characteristics: (a) low volatility (b) strong acidic character (c) strong affinity for water and (d) ability to act as an oxidising agent.
385+386 In aqueous solution, sulphuric acid ionises in two steps. H2SO4(aq) + H2O(l) → H3O^+(aq) + HSO4^–(aq); Ka1 = very large (Ka1 > 10). HSO4^–(aq) + H2O(l) → H3O^+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq); Ka2 = 1.2 × 10^–2
386 The larger value of Ka1 (Ka1 > 10) means that H2SO4 is largely dissociated into H^+ and HSO4^–.
702+703 The ionisation of HSO4^– to H3O^+ and SO4^2– is very very small. That is why Ka2 << Ka1.
388 The acid forms two series of salts: normal sulphates (such as sodium sulphate and copper sulphate) and acid sulphates (e.g., sodium hydrogen sulphate).
389 Sulphuric acid, because of its low volatility can be used to manufacture more volatile acids from their corresponding salts. 2MX + H2SO4 → 2HX + M2SO4 (X = F, Cl, NO3) (M = Metal)
417 CaF2 + H2SO4 → CaSO4 + 2HF
389+390 Concentrated sulphuric acid is a strong dehydrating agent. Many wet gases can be dried by passing them through sulphuric acid, provided the gases do not react with the acid.
391+392 Sulphuric acid removes water from organic compounds; it is evident by its charring action on carbohydrates. C12H22O11 → 12C + 11H2O (H2SO4)
392+393 Hot concentrated sulphuric acid is a moderately strong oxidising agent. In this respect, it is intermediate between phosphoric and nitric acids.
394 Both metals and non-metals are oxidised by concentrated sulphuric acid, which is reduced to SO2. Cu + 2H2SO4(conc.) → CuSO4 + SO2 + 2H2O; S + 2H2SO4(conc.) → 3SO2 + 2H2O; C + 2H2SO4(conc.) → CO2 + 2SO2 + 2H2O
394 A nation's industrial strength can be judged by the quantity of sulphuric acid it produces and consumes.
396 The bulk of sulphuric acid produced is used in the manufacture of fertilisers (e.g., ammonium sulphate, superphosphate).
397 Other uses are in: (a) petroleum refining (b) manufacture of pigments, paints and dyestuff intermediates (c) detergent industry (d) metallurgical applications (e.g., cleansing metals before enameling, electroplating and galvanising (e) storage batteries (f) in the manufacture of nitrocellulose products and (g) as a laboratory reagent.
